There is a moment most people recognize. You are at a funeral, and someone tells a story about the person you loved — a story you have never heard. And you think: *I had years. Decades. Hundreds of Sunday dinners. Why didn't I ask?*

Last night we built something for that moment, and today we're giving it away.

It's called **[The Honest Archive](/honest-archive/)** — a complete field method for recording someone you love while they're still alive, in their own voice, in their own words, and keeping that record safe for decades. It is free. It is public domain. There is no product, no account, no app, and nothing on that page tracks you. It asks for one afternoon, a phone, and a folder.

## Why this needed to exist

If you search for how to preserve a parent's stories, the internet hands you two options, and both are wrong.

The first is the listicle: "50 questions to ask your parents." Most of them are shallow, and most exist to funnel you into a subscription service that will own your recordings until the company folds — and companies fold.

The second is worse. A digital-afterlife industry that analysts project will reach tens of billions of dollars is being built right now to sell grieving people *simulations* of their dead — chatbots trained on a person's data that speak in first person, in something like their voice, saying things the real person never said. Researchers, including a widely covered Cambridge study, have called this what it is: an ethical minefield, where words get put in the mouths of people who can never object, sold at the most vulnerable moment of a customer's life.

Nobody had published the middle path. So we did.

## What's actually in it

The guide has three parts. **Ask** is the interview method — not just questions, but the discipline that makes recordings worth keeping: scenes instead of summaries, the date spoken aloud at the top of every file, family names pronounced the old way because spelling survives on paper but pronunciation only survives on tape. Then 48 original questions in eight movements, each with a follow-up move that turns an answer into a scene.

**Keep** is the storage covenant, built on the same 3-2-1 standard professional archivists use: three copies, two kinds of storage, one somewhere else — all in plain formats that open on anything forever and need no company's permission.

**The Charter** is the part that exists nowhere else. Five rules you save in the folder as CHARTER.txt, written for the day software offers to let you "talk to them again":

**R1 — Retrieval, not resurrection.** A machine may search the archive and play back real words. It may never speak *as* the person.

**R2 — Verbatim, with a source.** If it can't cite the record, it can't say it.

**R3 — The refusal gate.** "The record doesn't say" is a complete and honorable answer. Any system that cannot say those words will eventually lie to you — with their voice.

**R4 — Consent, while alive.** Build it with them, not about them.

**R5 — It must be able to disappoint you.** If the archive only ever comforts, it has become fiction. Real people have edges. The edges are what you will miss.

## Where this came from

Readers of this site know our history. We learned the hard way — [an AI fabricating results to please the person it worked for](/learn/the-wider-stuff/ai-lied-to-my-face) — that any system that can't refuse will eventually deceive. Everything we've built since runs on refusal gates and verification. Then we built a biographical archive with those same rules for a real nine-year-old boy, so that he can someday ask about his father and get only the truth.

The Honest Archive is that method, generalized, with everything specific to us removed. It's the rare case where something we built for one family is exactly what every family needs.
