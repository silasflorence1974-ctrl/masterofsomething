What we ruled out, and how we knew

Every star in the graveyard is a star we looked at carefully and decided was not hiding a planet. That is not a wasted night. Finding out that something is NOT what you hoped is most of what science actually is — and the only reason we would ever believe a real one is that we killed all these first.

Each reason below is a check the pipeline runs. If a check fails, the star is out.

the dip wasn't clear enough to trust
711 stars

Killed 711 stars so far. This is the most common way out.

Starlight is never perfectly steady. Cameras wobble, stars flicker, and if you stare at pure noise long enough you will always find a pattern that looks like something. SDE is a score for how much a dip stands out from the ordinary jitter of that star. A low score means: yes, there is a pattern there, but it is not louder than the noise, so believing it would be believing nothing.

The idea to keep: seeing a shape is not the same as the shape being real.

a second method didn't agree
626 stars

Killed 626.

We search for the dip with one algorithm, then search again with a completely different one written by different people. If the two disagree about how long the planet's year is, one of them is wrong and we don't get to pick which. Out.

The idea to keep: if you only have one way of measuring something, you don't actually know it yet.

*(This check was broken for seven hours on 2026-08-14 and quietly said "not sure" about 57 stars in a row instead of saying "I'm broken." We found it and fixed it. That is in this file on purpose.)*

the shadow lasted the wrong amount of time
592 stars

Killed 592.

Physics is strict here. If you know how long the planet's year is and how big the star is, you can calculate how many hours the crossing must take. If the real dip lasts far longer or far shorter than the math allows, then whatever made the dip was not a planet crossing in front of that star.

The idea to keep: you can often rule something out with arithmetic before you ever look closer.

the dips took turns being deeper
208 stars

Killed 208.

Number the dips: first, second, third, fourth. A planet is the same planet every time, so every dip should be the same depth. If the odd-numbered dips are deeper than the even-numbered ones, you are not watching one thing pass in front of a star — you are watching two stars orbiting each other, taking turns passing in front of one another. Two stars is a great thing to find. It just isn't a planet.

The idea to keep: the pattern in which measurements differ can tell you what you are actually looking at.

whatever it is, it's too big to be a planet
173 stars

Killed 173.

How much light disappears tells you how big the object is compared to its star. Past a certain size an object is not a planet anymore — it is a failed star, or a real star. Our cutoff is 2.5 times the size of Jupiter. Anything bigger is out of the planet business.

The idea to keep: measure the thing before naming the thing.

it only happened once or twice
121 stars

Killed 121.

One dip is an event. Two is a coincidence. Three or more at exactly even spacing is an orbit. With too few crossings we cannot tell a planet from a passing asteroid, a cosmic ray hitting the camera, or a fluke.

The idea to keep: a claim about something that repeats needs to be shown repeating.

we caught the object's own light
60 stars

Killed 60.

Halfway between crossings, the object goes behind the star. If the total light drops again then — even slightly — the object is bright enough to be contributing its own light, which planets mostly aren't. That is a small hot star, not a world.

The idea to keep: look for what should happen at the other half of the orbit, not just the part you were watching.

somebody already found it
20 stars

Killed 20.

We check every survivor against the catalogs of planets already discovered and already being investigated. If it is in there, it is real — and it is theirs. We log it as a good sign that our pipeline works and we move on.

The idea to keep: rediscovering something known is not failure. It is your instrument passing its test.

These are the slow, careful ones, run after a star survives everything above.

the star itself was breathing

Some stars swell and shrink on their own, on a rhythm, all by themselves. That makes a dip in the light that is not a shadow of anything. TIC 296620403 — our most promising signal so far — died exactly this way on 2026-08-14. It looked like a planet with a 16-hour year. It was a star with a heartbeat.

the dip came from a different star

The camera pixel is bigger than the star. If the center of the light shifts sideways during the dip, the flicker is coming from a neighboring star that our pixel is also catching, not from our target at all.

the shape of the whole orbit

Watch the brightness across the entire orbit, not just the dips. Two stars whirling around each other stretch each other into eggs and beam light in ways a planet never could. A flat curve between dips is a point in a planet's favor.

The honest scoreboard

Nights run: 32. Stars searched: 968. Survivors that are still standing: one. Five signals survived their own night and were later killed by a check we added afterward.

That last number is the important one, and it should never be hidden. We got excited five times and were wrong five times, and each time the reason we found out was that we built a new way to be proven wrong. If we ever put a star on this page and say "this one might be real," this graveyard is the whole reason you should believe us.

Corrections

Things this project got wrong and then fixed. They are part of the record; a history with the mistakes removed is an advertisement.

  • A dead check reported as a careful one. The BLS cross-check crashed and returned no verdict for six hours. Because a crashed check and a cautious check both produce "inconclusive", it was tallied as a finding rather than a fault. It was found from a lint warning, not from anything watching the pipeline.
  • Skips counted as results. A star we failed to look at was being reported alongside stars we looked at and learned nothing from. Those are different facts. A skip is a to-do list; it is not a measurement.
  • A recount. Totals are now rebuilt from the night logs on every load rather than incremented. A running tally that only ever grows cannot be checked against anything; if a log were deleted, these numbers would go down.
  • Undecidable signals presented as surviving ones. A check that abstained for want of an input used to fall through to "still standing". One 8%-deep eclipsing binary sat in that column looking like a candidate. Abstentions are now a separate state, and the missing datum is named on each one.
  • A guard that suppressed a true result. A safeguard written to prevent false kills was withdrawing a correct one, which promoted the object rather than protecting it. Safeguards may now only weaken a claim, never strengthen it.
