# THE HONEST ARCHIVE

### How to keep someone you love — without inventing them.

**Retrieval, not resurrection.**

This is a free method, not a product. There is nothing to buy, no account to
make, no app to install. It is public domain: copy it, print it, translate it,
give it away. It asks for one afternoon, a phone, and a folder.

---

## THE MOMENT

There is a moment most people recognize. You are at a funeral, and someone
tells a story about the person you loved — a story you have never heard. And
you think: *I had years. Decades. Hundreds of Sunday dinners. Why didn't I
ask?*

The voice goes first. People keep old voicemails for years just to hear a
person say an ordinary sentence. The stories go next — not the big ones, which
get retold, but the texture: what the kitchen smelled like, how they said your
grandmother's name, the joke they made when the car wouldn't start.

You cannot keep a person. You can keep their words, in their voice, in their
own order. That is what an archive is. This guide shows you how to make one —
and how to protect it, forever, from the one thing that can ruin it: invention.

Because a new industry is being built right now that will offer, for a monthly
fee, to let you "talk to them again." It will be fluent, comforting, and false.
It will put sentences in their mouth that they never said. This guide is the
alternative. Everything here ends in their real words or in honest silence —
nothing in between.

## PART ONE — ASK

*The sessions. A phone's voice-memo app is enough.*

**Record voice, not just notes.** The transcript is the index; the voice is the
person. Tone carries half of what a human means.

**Say the date out loud at the top of every recording,** and both your names.
"It's July tenth, 2026, I'm sitting with my mother, Mary Florence." Thirty
seconds of ceremony that turns a file into a record.

**Ask for scenes, not summaries.** When they say "we were poor but happy," ask:
*what did you eat on a good week? Where were you standing when the rent was
short?* A memory with a floor under it is twice as alive.

**Verbatim is treasure.** The phrases they always say — get them said properly,
on the record. Names, too: have them speak the full names of the people they
came from, pronounced the way the old country pronounced them. Spelling
survives on paper; pronunciation only survives on tape.

**Tangents outrank the list.** The questions below are matches, not rails. If
one question lights a forty-minute story you never planned, you have won.

**Keep sessions short.** Forty-five minutes to an hour, then stop — even
mid-story. Many short sessions beat one exhausting marathon, and "we'll pick it
up there next week" is how you get a next week.

**Interview them alone, and together.** Couples and siblings correct each other
into the truth. But some things are only said one-on-one. Do both.

**Don't rescue them from silence.** When a hard question lands, count to ten
before you speak. The tape can hold a silence. What comes after one is usually
the reason you came.

**Keep the contradictions.** If what they say today disagrees with what they
said last month, keep both, dated. People are allowed to disagree with
themselves twenty years apart. That is not an error in the record — that *is*
the person.

## THE QUESTIONS

*Forty-eight, in eight movements. You will not use them all. Each movement ends
with a follow-up move — the technique that turns an answer into a scene.*

### I. BEFORE YOU

1. Tell me about a day you remember from before I was born — an ordinary one.
2. What did your childhood kitchen smell like? Who cooked, and what was the
   dish that meant things were okay?
3. Where did you sleep as a child? Describe the room at night.
4. What were you afraid of at eight years old?
5. What is the first money you ever earned, and what did you do with it?
6. Who was your best friend when you were twelve? Tell me one specific thing
   the two of you did.

*The move: ask where they were standing. Ask what they were wearing. The
details feel trivial; they are the difference between a fact and a memory.*

### II. THE WORLD THEY CAME FROM

7. What did your parents' voices sound like? Say something the way your mother
   used to say it.
8. What was a rule your family lived by that was never said out loud?
9. What did your family argue about?
10. Who in the family were you supposed to take after — and did you?
11. What is a story your grandparents told that you can still tell?
12. Say the full names of the people you came from — and how they were really
    pronounced.

*The move: get the names in their voice. This is the recording their
great-grandchildren will play.*

### III. LOVE AND CHOICES

13. Tell me about a door you almost walked through — the job, the person, the
    city that almost happened.
14. How did you meet the person you loved? What were you wearing? What was the
    weather?
15. What did you get wrong about love the first time?
16. When did you know you would stay? Was there a moment?
17. What is a fight you are glad you had?
18. What did you give up — and was it worth it?

*The move: when they summarize — "we just hit it off" — ask for the first ten
minutes.*

### IV. WORK AND CRAFT

19. Walk me through a workday from your thirties, from the alarm to lights-out.
20. What could you do that most people around you couldn't?
21. Who taught you your work? What did their hands look like?
22. What is the best thing you ever made or did that nobody applauded?
23. What did a paycheck mean in the year money was tightest?
24. If you had one afternoon to teach me a single skill, which one — and how
    would you start?

*The move: ask for the tools by name. Vocabulary is a fingerprint.*

### V. THE HARD CHAPTERS

25. What is the heaviest thing you have carried that I might not know about —
    if you are willing to say.
26. When were you most afraid? What did you do the next morning?
27. What do you regret — and what do you refuse to regret, even though people
    expect you to?
28. Who did you lose first? What do you still have of theirs?
29. What did you believe at twenty that turned out to be wrong?
30. What got you through — the sentence, the person, the habit?

*The move: silence. Count to ten. The tape can hold it.*

### VI. BELIEFS AND DOUBTS

31. What do you believe happens after this? How sure are you?
32. What is something you changed your mind about late in life — and what
    changed it?
33. Where do you disagree with me — really?
34. What was your biggest doubt in the role that mattered most to you?
35. What is a conviction you want to outlive you — in your exact words?
36. What question do you wish someone had asked you?

*The move: let them contradict last session. Keep both takes. Date them.*

### VII. THE ORDINARY DAY

37. What does a perfect ordinary Saturday look like? Not a vacation — a good
    normal day.
38. What do you always say — the phrases everyone teases you about? Say them
    properly, for the record.
39. What is your order? The coffee, the sandwich, the usual.
40. Hum the song you hum without noticing. Sing a line of it.
41. What are your hands doing when you are happy?
42. Describe the room you are in right now, as if to someone fifty years from
    now.

*The move: this movement feels skippable. It is not. Later, nobody aches for a
résumé — they ache for a Tuesday.*

### VIII. LAST THINGS

43. What do you want people to know you forgave — or were forgiven for?
44. Is there something you never got to say to someone specific? Say it now,
    with their name in it.
45. What do you hope I keep — an object, a habit, a day of the year?
46. What should be told at your funeral — and what is nobody's business?
47. What do you want the youngest ones, and the ones not yet born, to hear
    from you directly?
48. Is there anything you want on the record that I didn't know to ask?

*The move: end every session with number 48. It catches everything you didn't
know to ask.*

## PART TWO — KEEP

*The archive. A folder of plain files you own, protected by three copies.*

**The 3-2-1 rule** — the standard professional archivists use: keep **three**
copies of everything, on **two** different kinds of storage, with **one** of
them somewhere else (another house, a safe deposit box, a trusted cloud). A
fire, a flood, or a stolen laptop should never be able to take all three.

**Plain formats only.** Audio as MP3 or WAV. Transcripts as plain text (.txt or
.md). Photos as JPEG. Documents as PDF. These open on anything, forever, and
need no company's permission. If a service holds your recordings, it must let
you export everything — and export it anyway, tonight. Companies die. A folder
of plain files does not.

**Name files so they sort themselves.** Date first:

    2026-07-10_mary-florence_childhood.mp3
    2026-07-10_mary-florence_childhood.txt

**Keep a MANIFEST.** One plain-text file at the top of the folder that says
what exists. Start it like this:

    THE ARCHIVE OF: Mary Florence, b. 1948
    STARTED BY: her son David, July 2026
    WHAT THIS IS: her life, in her own voice, in her own words.
    RULES: see CHARTER.txt in this folder. Retrieval, not resurrection.

    2026-07-10  childhood, the Cork kitchen, her father's boots   41 min
    2026-07-24  meeting Dad, the almost-move to Boston            52 min

**Transcribe, but keep the audio.** Any transcription tool is fine. The
transcript is for finding things. The voice is the point.

**Print the best of it.** Choose the twenty pages you would save from a fire
and print them on paper. Paper needs no electricity, no format migration, and
no password, and it has a five-hundred-year track record.

**The birthday check.** Once a year, on their birthday, open the folder. Play
one file to make sure it still plays. Copy anything new to all three places.
Ten minutes of maintenance, disguised as a ritual you will actually keep.

## PART THREE — THE CHARTER

*Put this in the folder as CHARTER.txt. It is the part of this guide that does
not exist anywhere else — the rules that keep the archive honest after you can
no longer ask.*

Someday — sooner than you think — software will offer to let you "talk to them
again." It will read everything in your folder and speak back in something like
their voice, saying things they never said. It will be tender, fluent, and
fabricated, and it will be sold to you at the most vulnerable moment of your
life. Decide the rules now, while your head is clear and they are still here to
agree with you.

**R1 — RETRIEVAL, NOT RESURRECTION.** A machine may search this archive and
play back real words. It may never speak *as* this person — not in first
person, not in their voice, not "what they would have said." The dead do not
take new positions.

**R2 — VERBATIM, WITH A SOURCE.** Every answer must point to a recording or a
transcript, named and dated. If it cannot cite the record, it cannot say it.

**R3 — THE REFUSAL GATE.** "The record doesn't say" is a complete and honorable
answer. Any system that cannot say those words will eventually lie to you —
with their voice. The gaps in an archive are sacred; they are the shape of a
real person.

**R4 — CONSENT, WHILE ALIVE.** Build this with them, not about them. Let them
hear what is in it. Let them strike anything. Let them seal things —
"open this in twenty years" is a legitimate instruction, and it should be
honored to the day.

**R5 — IT MUST BE ABLE TO DISAPPOINT YOU.** If the archive only ever comforts,
it has become fiction. Keep the sharp opinions, the wrong predictions, the
unfair judgments, the contradictions. Real people have edges. The edges are
what you will miss.

Fabricated comfort is theft twice over: it steals words from someone who cannot
object, and it steals your grief from you — leaving you to mourn a fiction that
was easier company than the person actually was. Honest silence keeps faith
with both of you.

## THE ONE-HOUR VERSION

*For the night you find this at 2 a.m. and cannot do all of it.*

1. Tonight: open the voice-memo app on your phone.
2. Sit with them. Say the date and both your names out loud.
3. Ask question 1: *"Tell me about a day you remember from before I was born —
   an ordinary one."*
4. Follow the tangents. Stop after thirty or forty minutes.
5. Before you sleep: send the file to your own email, and copy it to one more
   place. Two copies tonight; the third this week.

Done is better than perfect. A single honest hour, saved twice, beats the
complete oral history you never quite start.
